# A long note

A note long enough to scroll, skip through and switch modes in while it reads. The text was written for this sandbox.

## The lighthouse keeper

The lighthouse stood at the end of a narrow spit of rock, and for most of the year the only way to reach it was by boat. The keeper, a patient woman named Ada, had lived there for eleven winters. She kept a log of every ship that passed, every change in the wind, and every repair she made to the great lamp.

In the mornings she climbed the spiral stairs, one hundred and twelve of them, and polished the lens until it shone. The lens was older than she was, made of curved glass prisms that bent the light into a single beam. When it turned at night, the beam swept across the water every ten seconds, and sailors miles away could count the flashes and know exactly where they were.

Supplies came once a fortnight, weather permitting. A small boat would appear at the edge of the bay, rising and falling on the swell, and Ada would walk down to the landing with a rope over her shoulder. The boatman rarely stayed long. He would hand up crates of food, oil for the lamp and a bundle of letters, then turn back before the tide changed.

## The storm

One autumn a storm arrived earlier than anyone expected. The sky turned the colour of slate by noon, and the sea began to heave against the rocks with a sound like distant thunder. Ada lit the lamp hours before dusk. She knew that ships caught out in such weather would be looking for the light, and that they would need it sooner than usual.

By evening the wind had grown so strong that the whole tower seemed to hum. Spray reached the gallery, sixty feet above the waves. Through the rain she saw the lights of a fishing boat, far too close to the reef, struggling to turn into the wind. She could do nothing but keep the lamp turning and hope its crew could read the flashes.

The boat turned at last. Ada watched its lights move slowly along the coast and round the headland into the shelter of the harbour. She wrote the time in her log, and beside it a single word: safe.

## The visitor

In spring a young surveyor came to measure the coast. He stayed for three days, sleeping in the store room and spending his evenings asking Ada questions. How did she know when the oil would run low? What did she do when the clockwork that turned the lens broke down? Had she ever thought of leaving?

She answered the first two questions carefully, showing him the gauges and the spare parts she kept in a wooden chest. The third question she answered with a shrug. The work mattered, she said, and someone had to do it. Besides, she liked the quiet, and the way the sea changed colour with the seasons.

When he left, the surveyor promised to send her a copy of his map. It arrived months later, rolled in a tube and tied with string. Her lighthouse was marked on it with a small star, and beside the star he had written her name.

## The new lamp

Years passed, and the lamp was replaced with an electric one. Engineers arrived with cables and generators, and within a week the old clockwork was silent. The new light was brighter and needed far less care. Ada found that her days, once full of small tasks, now had long empty stretches in them.

She filled them by walking the shore, collecting stones worn smooth by the sea, and writing down the stories the boatmen told her. Her log grew into a different kind of record: not ships and winds, but people, and the lives they lived along that stretch of coast.

## The end of the record

When Ada finally left the lighthouse, she took the log with her. It ran to fourteen volumes, each one bound in cloth and labelled by year. A museum in the town offered to keep them, and she agreed on one condition: that anyone who wished to read them could do so.

The volumes are still there. Visitors turn the pages carefully, reading about storms that blew out a century ago and ships long since broken up for scrap. And on one page, written in a steady hand, they find a time, the name of a fishing boat, and a single word: safe.
